*[Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/.../sylvestre-alphabet-album): “An ‘alphabet album,’ a beautiful book of calligraphy and typographic engraving from 1843 assembled by Joseph-Balthazar Silvestre. Ranging from the old-fashioned to the surprisingly modern looking, from the elegantly unreadable to the crystal clear.*

I wish I could tag [Joshua Darden](https://www.dardenstudio.com/about) on this. Looking at this, it occurred to me that there’s a whole history to be found in which direction this kind of dimensional typography is extruded in. Are we looking down on it (as this example) or up at it? Is it trailing away to the left or right? Is it deep or shallow? Does it cast a shadow on another surface or is it floating? Are the dimensional extrusions straight or curved, like the presentation type for Superman? If you could vacuum up every example from the last few centuries and wrap them in a bit of metadata — basically, “history, form, and use” and someone I knew once put it — you’d have some very fertile soil for a speculative history. I say *speculative* because the data would be so thin, the role of arbitrary aesthetics so central, and the effect of trends (like superhero comics) would be so strong that there wouldn’t be any way to ‘prove’ some neatly reductive thesis. BUT: all of those factors would be prominent because the broad context would be secular modernity. And that matters because we have a few vast and meticulously documented control groups. The most important one is the entire history of lettering in religious texts — illuminations, initials, and all the rest. And while that entire field marks a millennium-long high point of utterly fantastic ‘ludic‘ history, you can be sure that ‘stylistic’ questions of the kind I’m asking would have been seen as having serious theological significance. (Just one concrete example: Jan Van Eyk’s Annunciation in NYC’s Met, in which the angel Gabriel’s message is placed ‘forward’ in space but Mary’s response is ‘backwards’![!!] 😹) And, indeed, there’s another control group as well: classical monumental inscriptions from a society — many, really — where religion was pluralistic, so there would be no need to attribute some ridiculous theological implication to how an artist positioned their perspective on words. I had some other thought about this, but it escapes me now. Worth revisiting, though, as one of those non-projects I’d get to if I had more discipline. Then again, if I did I wouldn’t have thought of this, let alone written it down. 😜

\[\[Ah! I remember that thing: The formalization of this kind of dimensionality in Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines — in plain terms, the precise specs that define how things like windows / buttons / etc cast shadows: “top-left lighting,” 45° angle above the interface plane. AFAIK, prior to Apple’s HIG it (a) didn’t exist as a subject and (b) was anything goes. Making that consistent was crucial for establishing the implies light source that made a consistent UI the modern equivalent of Renaissance perspective. I’m pretty sure Steve Jobs just dictated it based on his own preferences, but that doesn’t mean his choices weren’t freighted with a bit of cultural baggage we could open and rummage through.]]
