From “[To Live and Love with a Dying World: A conversation between Tim DeChristopher and Wendell Berry](https://orionmagazine.org/article/to-live-and-love-with-a-dying-world/),” in. *Orion* magazine (March 2, 2020):

> **Wendell Berry**: It was the Shakers who were sure the end could come anytime, and they still saved the seeds and figured out how to make better diets for old people. Thomas Merton was interested in the Shakers. I said to him, “If they were certain that the world could end at any minute, how come they built the best building in Kentucky?”
> 
> “You don’t understand,” he said. “If you know the world could end at any minute, you know there’s no need to hurry. You take your time and do the best work you possibly can.” That was important to me. I’ve repeated it many times.
> 
> **Tim De Christopher**: That’s why the despair is not paralyzing. Knowing that it’s too late to prevent collapse, knowing that we’re not going to stop the catastrophic end, knowing that we’re going to die — it doesn’t mean that we stop. It means that we live in this moment as fully as we can.

I was fortunate beyond words to have learned from some brilliant historians when I was young — [Tom Generous](https://www.legacy.com/us/obituaries/newsobserver/name/william-generous-obituary?id=37122402) in high school, then [Holly Hendrix](https://utsnyc.edu/blog/2014/12/02/holland-lee-hendrix-14th-president-of-union-dies-at-66/), [Alan Segal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_F._Segal), [Morton Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morton_Smith), [Richard Landes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Landes), [Nina Garsoian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nina_Garso%C3%AFan), and [Caroline Walker Bynum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caroline_Walker_Bynum) in college. In particular, I was interested in the history of the apocalypse, that millennia-old nothingburger that human civilization orbits around, like a dark star. The biggest thing I took from all that was that *periodization is everything*. It is to the past what genre is to how we externalize the immaterial things inside our heads, hearts, hands, and all the rest; indeed, the period / era / epoch / etc *is* the native genre of history in all that (anglophone) word’s ambiguity. So, in my view, pretty much everything — culture, so society, politics, economy, etc — “is downstream of” how we slice and dice our relationship to time, very much including what some call the future. (You read that right: our sense of time ‘precedes’ even culture — because for most of human pre/history, “the past” didn’t exist. As any fool can see, the present — *pace* William Gibson — *is* the past.) If there’s one thing I’ve spent my entire adult life pondering, it’s the effects of how we periodize things — how those imaginary periods, reified and alienated from us and us from them, shape every aspect of what we do. So it’s not so often that some idea comes along that completely topples something I’ve assumed for much of my life. But this is one — and this is it. Who’d have thought that Wendell Barry would make Agamben sound like another one of those (as Deleuze put it) *state philosophers*?
